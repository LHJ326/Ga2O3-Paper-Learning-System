# A Compute-in-Memory Annealing Processor With Interaction Coefficient Reuse and Sparse Energy Computation for Solving Combinatorial Optimization Problems

Yifeng Zhou , Graduate Student Member, IEEE, Guocheng Su, Student Member, IEEE, Jinrong Zhou, Student Member, IEEE, Lei Liao , Senior Member, IEEE, and Zhuojun Chen , Senior Member, IEEE

**Abstract— Since combinatorial optimization problems (COPs) are a class of non-deterministic polynomial-time (NP)-hard problems, it is impracticable to solve them in brute-force searches, which results in high energy consumption and long computation latency. The annealing processors based on the Ising model are naturally oriented to find approximate solutions. However, these processors face the challenges of frequent data movement between computing elements and memory units, resulting in significantly large area and high energy consumption. To address these issues, we present a fully digital annealing processor based on compute-in-memory (CIM) architecture. To enhance area efficiency, a CIM coefficient array is designed with an interaction coefficient reuse strategy. Moreover, a sparsity-aware adder tree is proposed to reduce unnecessary add operations, which can improve the energy efficiency. For searching the lowest energy state of the Ising model, a nonlinear probability flipping (NPF) approximate circuit is designed, which is based on a voting mechanism and on-chip random number generation with low hardware overhead. The proposed annealing processor is fabricated in a 55-nm CMOS process and used to solve the max-cut problem as well as the image segmentation problem. The measured results confirm the high energy efficiency (2.4 fJ at 0.9 V per spin) and the high area efficiency (402 µm**<strong><sup>2</sup></strong> **per spin).**

**Index Terms— Annealing processors, combinatorial optimization problems (COPs), compute-in-memory (CIM), image segmentation, Ising model, max-cut problem.**

## B. Prior Works of the CMOS Annealing Processor

Dupret et al. [18] proposed a simulated annealing processor using a Lattice graph topology and an analog computation method for local energy calculation. CMOS annealing processors can afford to high topological complexity and large coefficient bit width, which have been demonstrated in [19], [20], [21], and [22].

Yamaoka et al. [19] proposed an annealing processor with a topology complexity of the nearest five neighbors (adding a vertical connection relationship based on the Lattice graph) and a coefficient bit width of 2 bits. To enable the on-chip annealing feature, random numbers had to be generated externally or the memory supply voltage had to be lowered to avoid getting stuck at the local minimum state.

Takemoto et al. [20] implemented an annealing processor design with 3-bit coefficients. However, the coefficients had to be read out from the memory array before the local energy was calculated, which increased the annealing calculation cycle and severely affected the overall energy efficiency of the annealing computation.

To address these issues, several annealing processors based on the CIM architecture were proposed. Xie et al. [21]

developed a CIM annealing processor in the analog domain, where all computations were performed on the bit lines of the memory array. However, the spin states were required to be read out using low-offset sense amplifiers (SAs), and careful design was needed to ensure its robustness to process, voltage, and temperature (PVT) variations.

Su et al. [22] proposed a digital CIM annealing processor that used registers to store spin states, allowed spin to be updated within a single cycle, and achieved high computation parallelism. However, both of these designs [21], [22] required an external source of random number, which still incurred significant hardware overhead. Furthermore, there was a lack of sparsity strategy for the local energy computation, resulting in redundant calculations and excessive energy consumption.

## III. ANNEALING PROCESSOR

## A. Full-Chip Architecture

The overall architecture of the proposed annealing processor is illustrated in Fig. 3, which is composed of 36 PEs, wordline (WL) and bitline (BL) drivers, and spin state output drivers. For King’s graph connection, each PE can perform spin state annealing computation for 25 spins, resulting in a total of 900 spins at the system level. Each PE consists of a coefficient array, a sparsity-aware adder tree, a spin state storage register array, a random number control circuit, an NPF approximate circuit, an annealing control circuit, and an SRAM read/write peripheral circuit. In addition, adjacent PEs are interconnected through spin state interface circuits to provide the overall King’s graph connection. Multiple top-level PEs enable the processor to meet the requirements of different scale COPs feasibly. Section III provide a comprehensive description of the detailed circuit design.

## D. Coefficient Reuse Strategy

In order to improve the storage efficiency, we propose a coefficient reuse strategy. For the LR array, LD array, and RD array, we adopt an odd–even grouping reuse strategy. For the UD array, we use a UD array coefficient reuse strategy based on XNOR.

1) Odd–Even Grouping Reuse Strategy: As shown in Fig. 7(a), the coefficients of the LR array, LD array, and RD array are grouped according to the column numbers, ranging from 0 to 5. The odd and even columns are grouped together. Taking the LR array as an example, in the first computation cycle, col⟨0⟩ of the even group is regarded as left connection coefficients, and col⟨1⟩ of the odd group is regarded as right connection coefficients. Thus, the corresponding columns are used to complete the spin state update. In the second computation cycle, col⟨2⟩ of the even group is regarded as right connection coefficients, and col⟨1⟩ of the odd group is regarded as left connection coefficients. By utilizing col⟨1⟩ of the odd group twice, we can reuse the stored coefficients.

For the LD array and the RD array, the coefficient reuse strategy is the same, as shown in Fig. 7(b). However, multiplexers are required at the output ports of the RBL signals. It is because the same column serves as the left (right) diagonal coefficients during the first computation cycle. Among 24 RBL signals, the first (last) 20 RBL signals represent the array computation results. However, during the second computation cycle, the same column serves as the right (left) diagonal coefficients, and the last (first) 20 RBL signals represent the array computation results.

2) UD Array Coefficient Reuse Strategy: As shown in Fig. 8, in order to reduce the hardware overhead of computing resources, XNOR operations are performed only with up spin connection for the UD array. The computation results of down

## F. NPF Approximate Circuit

NPF is an effective method to prevent the system from being trapped in local energy minima. The commonly used model for NPF function is Gibbs model, which has been discussed in Section II.

Implementing NPF with transcendental function calculation requires numerous computing resources. Therefore, we propose an approximate circuit for NPF function based on a voting mechanism. The two main features of the NPF function are as follows.

1) At the initial stage of the annealing process which has a high temperature, the next spin state is closely related to the calculated local energy. The higher the local calculated energy, the higher the probability of the spin state flipping to the state with the same sign bit of the local energy.

2) As the annealing process progresses, the temperature gradually decreases, and the next spin state becomes closely related to the temperature. In the final stage of the annealing process, the spin state is directly determined by the sign bit of the calculated local energy.

## G. Embedded Random Number Generator

In previous works, a dedicated RNG is required to generate random numbers for random probability flipping. There are several common approaches for random number generation, such as linear feedback shift register (LFSR) [30] and cellular automata shift register (CASR) [31]. However, these methods occupy a large chip area. In this article, a set of random number sequences with an almost 50% probability of ones is required as the basis for probability flipping.

The size of transistors in SRAM cell and SA is optimized to meet the requirement of XNOR calculation, coefficient storage, and random number generation through Monte Carlo simulations. When the test chip is powered up, the SRAM cell is initially in a metastable state. At this time, the read operation is performed directly on the coefficient array. The metastable state is amplified by SA and a random number sequence can be generated, which is uniquely dependent on the process variations. Then 22.08-kb random bits are read out from un-touched SRAM arrays to obtain the randomness and stored in the field-programmable gate array (FPGA). Considering that each NPF only requires 35 bits, the number of random bits is sufficient. Thus, we only read the memory array once. Once all random numbers have been read, the coefficients are written into the SRAM array. During testing, we control the FPGA to send the generated random numbers into the chip through an interface circuit (serial-to-parallel data converter). After completing one NPF, a new random number sequence is fed into the voter during the local search phase.
